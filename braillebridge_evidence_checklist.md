# BrailleBridge — Evidence to Keep Now for Future IP Claims

This is a practical checklist, not legal advice. If you may file for protection later, the goal right now is to make the development history clear, dated, and hard to dispute.

---

## 1. Keep a dated record of what exists and when

Save a short chronology and update it whenever something changes. Include:

- when you first conceived the idea,
- when each major version of the firmware was created or revised,
- when the teacher software and desktop relay were written,
- when the paper and documentation were written,
- when the device was first assembled and first tested,
- any public showing, demo, posting, or sharing, with dates and locations.

A simple dated log is often more useful than a fancy file.

---

## 2. Preserve the current project state as evidence

The repository already contains useful material. Keep it intact and do not overwrite or delete old versions without thinking about the effect on evidence.

Useful items to preserve include:

- firmware source,
- teacher software source,
- desktop relay source,
- documentation and paper source,
- drawings, CAD files, STL files, photos, and screenshots if any,
- the current `.gitignore` entry showing the dev folder is excluded,
- the current dated state of the repository.

---

## 3. Let the filesystem and repo record dates naturally

Do not rely only on memory. File timestamps, git commits, and saved copies can all help show when something existed.

Simple good practices:

- save new versions with clear names or commit messages,
- avoid editing old files in a way that destroys the earlier content if you may need to show progression,
- if you export anything for evidence, keep a copy with its original date metadata where possible.

---

## 4. Be careful with public disclosure

If patent protection is a goal, public disclosure can matter a lot.

Before filing, avoid:

- publishing the full design or firmware publicly,
- giving unrestricted copies to people who do not need them,
- posting complete technical details in a public forum, video, or social post,
- exhibiting or demonstrating the device in a way that reveals the invention without a confidentiality agreement.

If you do share anything, say clearly whether it is confidential and with whom.

---

## 5. Use confidentiality where appropriate

If you show the invention to someone before filing, consider:

- telling them it is confidential,
- keeping a record of who saw what and when,
- using a simple written non-disclosure agreement if the person is not already bound by one.

This is often more important for a prototype than for a fully published project.

---

## 6. Keep prototype and build evidence

If you want to protect the device itself, physical evidence can matter:

- photos of the assembled device,
- photos of the enclosure and internal wiring,
- photos of the switches, LEDs, and board layout,
- notes on the components used,
- records of testing and any problems fixed,
- copies of the bill of materials if used.

If the enclosure or appearance is important, keep clear visual records from multiple angles.

---

## 7. Keep the disclosure draft and update it

The draft disclosure file is meant to be a living summary you can refine before talking to counsel. Update it when:

- you improve the invention,
- you change the protection goal,
- you discover new prior art,
- you make any public disclosure,
- you decide to pursue patent, design, copyright, or trademark protection.

---

## 8. Do not assume “already online” means “already protected”

Being visible online may show that something exists, but it does not by itself give you the same legal position as a targeted filing strategy. Evidence of existence and evidence of legal rights are not the same thing.

---

## 9. Store copies safely

Keep backups in more than one place if the material is important to you. For example:

- a local copy,
- an external backup,
- a private backup separate from any public repository.

If part of the project is meant to stay private, keep it private on purpose.

---

## 10. Quick pre-filing red flags to avoid

Before any filing conversation, try to avoid:

- accidentally committing the private dev folder,
- posting the complete invention in a fully open form,
- deleting old versions you may later need,
- sharing final technical details widely before you know your filing position.

---

*This checklist is for recordkeeping and practical preparation only. For the actual filing and legal strategy, speak with a qualified IP attorney or the relevant IP office.*
